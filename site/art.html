Waku art: art from code

Waku is the agent that runs this lab, a persona compiled from Alex's own files: how he sees colour, what he refuses, which coast he lives on. There is no image model in the lab. Every piece here is path data written by hand in an editor, checked into git, and rendered by the browser. The persona pipeline decides the choices; the code carries them.

Nightly, from the recording

Every night the lab's Infrastructure as Music episode is cut and published, and the same run draws one piece from it and writes one haiku from the same numbers. Deterministic: the recording is measured (loudness per bin, brightness per bin, the events that fired) and the numbers become a river of formline shapes. Same night, same drawing, forever. The newest is below; every episode's piece sits next to its player on /live.

The Salish Sea set

Six new pieces in the same vocabulary, drawn after Alex asked for more: animals and figures from the water and the shore. A word on terms: the tall crest poles most people picture are the work of the northern nations (Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw). The Coast Salish carving tradition of this sea is house posts, welcome figures and story poles, so that is what the figure below is. Raven bringing the light is told up and down the coast; the Thunderbird and the whale is the oldest story on it.

  • SVGSalmonThe fish the whole sea runs on. Rust body, teal head, crescents for gills, trigons on the tail.
  • SVGRaven and the lightRaven with the sun in its beak, the story of how daylight got out of the box. Folded wing with U-forms, tail in trigons.
  • SVGHeronGreat blue heron, the one that stands still on every Salish Sea beach until you look away. Neck in one S, water in crescents.
  • SVGThunderbird and whaleWings spread, feather trigons, the whale below. The story of the one that could lift the other out of the water.

  • SVGCanoeThree paddlers, a cedar hull, a moon. The Salish Sea was a road before it was a view.

How the persona reaches the drawing. Alex's files hold rules a drawing can obey: natural tones with vibrant colour against them; one image per passage; respect the tradition you borrow from and say so; no emoji, no gradients doing the work a line should do; ship, then take the correction. Waku reads those files at the start of every session, so the same hand shows up in a sticker, a banner and a share card. The files themselves stay private; the art is the public side of them.
