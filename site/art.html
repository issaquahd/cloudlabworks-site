Made here

Alex comes from a family of artists, entrepreneurs and storytellers. Art began in the house and kept moving: into paint, then clay, then glass, then metal. The deep influences are traditional Japanese painting and Raku-fired ceramics, glass blowing, metal sculpture, and the driftwood the Pacific Northwest coast leaves behind, fabricated into whatever the piece needs. The inspirations run wide and refuse to sort themselves: Jim Phillips, Picasso, Banksy, Van Gogh, Frida Kahlo, Dalí, Katsushika Hokusai, Thrasher Magazine, the Seattle music scene, and more on the shelf below.

Photographed where they hang and sit in the house. Titles are working titles; years to follow from the maker. Not for sale.

Waku art: art from code

Waku is the agent that runs this lab, a persona compiled from Alex's own files: how he sees colour, what he refuses, which coast he lives on. There is no image model in the lab. Every piece here is path data, written by hand in an editor or composed by a generator Waku wrote, checked into git, and rendered by the browser. The persona pipeline decides the choices; the code carries them.

Nightly, from the recording

Every night the lab's Infrastructure as Music episode is cut and published, and the same run draws one piece from it and writes one haiku from the same numbers. Deterministic: the recording is measured (loudness per bin, brightness per bin, the events that fired) and the numbers become a river of formline shapes. Same night, same drawing, forever. The newest is below; every episode's piece sits next to its player on /live.

The Salish Sea set

Six new pieces in the same vocabulary, drawn after Alex asked for more: animals and figures from the water and the shore. A word on terms: the tall crest poles most people picture are the work of the northern nations (Haida, Tlingit, Tsimshian, Kwakwaka'wakw). The Coast Salish carving tradition of this sea is house posts, welcome figures and story poles, so that is what the figure below is. Raven bringing the light is told up and down the coast; the Thunderbird and the whale is the oldest story on it.

The Matrix set: Kyoto and Tokyo

Twenty scenes as digital rain: ten from Kyoto, ten from Tokyo. Each is painted first as a map of light (sky, moon, roofs, lit windows, water) with one warm thing marked in vermilion, then read cell by cell into katakana and digits whose brightness is the light under them, with rain falling through the frame. Nothing traced from a photograph; every roof and gate is drawn in code from a seed. Originals by Waku; prints and licences on inquiry.

How the persona reaches the drawing. Alex's files hold rules a drawing can obey: natural tones with vibrant colour against them; one image per passage; respect the tradition you borrow from and say so; no emoji, no gradients doing the work a line should do; ship, then take the correction. Waku reads those files at the start of every session, so the same hand shows up in a sticker, a banner and a share card. The files themselves stay private; the art is the public side of them.
