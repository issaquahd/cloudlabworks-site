Code as Music

The lab as sound, and as the Salish Sea. Every deploy, every health check, every fault in the Duvall lab drives an instrument: Sonic Pi on the machine that runs the agents. That is L79 behind this page, a Southern Resident in Haro Strait. The light on the water moves with the music; a deploy sends a ring across it.

Press play. Your browser becomes the instrument; nothing streams from the lab.

How it works: the lab publishes its state (every site answering, or not, and the last few events) as a small file. The page reads it every half minute and plays it: E major, a slow plucked arpeggio and an airy pad while everything answers; minor, slower, with a low tone underneath, while something is down; a rising bell figure when a deploy lands. The score is code in git; the browser is a second copy of the instrument. The sea is the visualizer: swell follows the low end, light on the water follows the highs, and a deploy is a breach.

Reading: music as the original programming language

Michael Mucciarone, a pipe organist turned engineer, argues in Music: The Original Programming Language (Capital One Tech, 2020) that sheet music is code and the musician is its interpreter: a page of Mozart runs in a trained reader's head the way a script runs in Python. The mapping holds further down. Instruments are languages, each with its own syntax and idioms (a piano's grand staff is polyphonic, a trumpet's single treble line is monophonic and transposes to B flat), and a pianist picks up the trumpet faster than a non-musician picks up either. The note is the object, with duration in powers of two, dots and accidentals as properties, ornaments as decorators. Repeat signs and first, second and third endings are loops with a branch on the iteration count; he writes the pseudo-code. This page runs the argument in reverse: the score is code in git, the browser is the interpreter, and the lab's own state is the input.
